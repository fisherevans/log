These words you're reading, they have my name attached to them.

And, if I'm being honest, I don't know how to feel about that.

Does it feel like I wrote them? To you? To me?

Did I type out every letter? Nope. Am I still the author? If so, am I bound by the meaning of these words in the same way I would be if I had hand-typed each letter? It's what's written down less authentic or legitimate depending on how is written?

I struggle putting my name on things, especially words that express beliefs, opinions, or ideas. Once my name is tied to something written down, it feels like a commitment. It feels like I am saying: this represents me. Permanently.

Once it's out there, it can be shared out of context. I can be misquoted. Readers can build up assumptions about who I am that might not be true.

That has always made writing difficult for me.

If someone (or something) helps me write, how much of the end result is still mine? Does that change if it was a friend who gave me feedback, or a professional editor? What about spell check? Grammarly? An LLM?

At what point does assistance become authorship? Do I need to identify AI as a co-author every time it touches something I eventually publish?

I really don't know.

This post is me trying to figure that out.

## Words feel permanent

I have always struggled with perceived commitment to beliefs and opinions. Not because I’m afraid to commit to things, but because putting an opinion into words makes it feel fixed. Like I’ve created a version of myself that I’m now responsible for defending.

If I write an opinion down, I feel like I need to be prepared to defend that opinion forever.

If I say something confidently and change my mind later, some part of my brain thinks I did something wrong.

Rationally, ***I know that's nonsense.***

Knowledge grows. Opinions change. Feelings evolve. People learn things. Being able to say, "I thought this before, and now I think something different" is a good thing.

But knowing that doesn't make the feeling of sharing my thoughts disappear.

When I'm writing, I can get stuck on a sentence because I don't know if that's *exactly* what I mean. Then I get stuck on the structure because I don't know if that's the order I want to make the argument. Then I start wondering if the argument is even valid. If my opinion is "right."

Eventually, I'll spend twenty minutes word-smithing a paragraph, and I still won't feel like it represents me.

Speaking feels different.

Especially when I don't think anyone is listening.

## Talking into the void

I have always had entire conversations in my head. I argue with myself. I imagine some vague persona disagreeing with me and then argue against the disagreement. I rehearse psuedo conversations that will probably never happen.

It's how I process my thoughts. How I form my opinions. I'm pretty sure that's just human nature. 

But actually discussing those same nuanced topics with others has always spiked my anxiety. Because of how polarized and reactionary society is these days; expressing a thought outloud begins to feel like defining your identity. I can't help but feel like some people think less of you if you try to see something from another point, especially if that alternative view disagrees with their own beliefs. 

So I don't. I've shied away from those conversations.

And then came agents and voice dictation. 

Voice dictation went from a joke, to nearly perfect, in a matter of months. It was suddenly good enough that I didn't need to constantly fight it for it to be useful.  These days, I dictate up to 10,000 words a day, mostly to my coding agents.

At work and at home, I use voice dictation to instruct my agents. I speak outloud, freely, about nuanced and technical problems. I ideate out loud. I debate the pros and cons of a decision verbally, and conversationally.

And I have no fear of judgement. 

And at some point I realized I was practicing something I had never really practiced before: expressing half-formed thoughts out loud.

Now I can actually argue against my own beliefs, out loud, without others feeling like their own beliefs are being attacked.

There is something freeing about talking into a void where there is no expectation that another person is judging the first version of the thought.

I can say something badly.

I can contradict myself five minutes later.

I can start explaining an idea and realize halfway through that I don't believe it.

Nothing has been published. Nobody has been convinced of anything. I haven't committed to a position.

I'm just thinking out loud.

And for whatever reason, my brain seems much more comfortable doing that than staring at an empty text box.

## Ramble

I ended up building a tool around this exact process that I call Ramble.

The goal isn't to build some facsimile of me that can manufacture blog posts while I go do something else. It isn't too build some replacement for human conversation. 

The goal is almost the opposite.

I want to give myself a way to put my thoughts into words. I wanted a way to be challenged without being worried that I'm being attacked. 

I can talk for twenty minutes, or an hour, or however long I have something to say. Ramble takes that material and organizes it. It can pull together the things I repeated. It can group ideas that belong together. It can point out where I contradicted myself or where there is an obvious counterargument I never addressed.

Most importantly, I have it preserve my own words and phrases as much as possible.

The output isn't the blog post.

It's a draft. Sometimes barely that. An outline, with some choice quotes.

It's something I can disagree with.

And that turns out to be extremely useful for me.

## Criticism is easier than creation

I am very quick to react to things. Often times too quick, and too critical.

My first instinct when I read an argument is usually to look for the hole in it. Where does this break? What assumption did this person make? What's missing? What would make this sentence more accurate? What ambiguities need to be clarified?

Ten times out of ten, the first sentence an agent writes for me will contain something I want to change.

And I use that.

Starting from nothing is hard for me. Starting from something that is wrong is much easier.

This is true well outside writing.

I don't tend to research something for three weeks, produce a perfect plan, and then execute it.

I jump in.

I make something.

Then I look at the thing I made and figure out why it sucks.

AI-generated RFCs work well for me for the same reason. A mediocre strawman gives me something to attack. Suddenly I know what matters because I know what I disagree with.

Ramble lets me do that with my own thoughts.

I can play both roles.

I can be the person trying to express something, and then I can be the asshole reviewing it.

Ramble isn't really a writing tool for me.

It's a thinking tool.

## So whose words are they?

This is where I get stuck.

Because none of that actually answers the uncomfortable question.

Once AI has touched the words, whose are they?

The test I keep coming back to is some combination of agency, judgment, and accountability.

Did I decide what I wanted to talk about?

Did the ideas come from something I actually believe or something I was genuinely trying to understand?

Did I decide what was good and bad, relevant and irrelevant, true and false?

Did I reject things that didn't represent me?

And, ultimately, am I willing to put my name on the finished thing and be responsible for what it says?

If the answer to those questions is yes, I have a hard time convincing myself that I am no longer the author just because an LLM helped me get there.

That doesn't mean the process doesn't matter.

If I type, "write me a thoughtful essay about AI and human connection," copy the result into my blog, and put my name on it, that feels fundamentally different to me.

The judgment happened somewhere else.

The exploration happened somewhere else.

The ideas may have happened somewhere else.

At that point I'm mostly making a publishing decision.

That isn't what I'm trying to do.

I want to end up with something I could defend. Something I recognize. Something where, if you ask me why a paragraph exists, I know.

Something where I read it afterward and think:

Yeah. That's me.

## We have never written alone

There is also a version of this concern that starts to become impossible if I follow it too far.

None of my ideas exist in isolation.

I talk to coworkers. I talk to friends. I read things. I hear a sentence six months ago that sticks somewhere in my brain and eventually comes back out with no attribution attached to it.

Everyone does this.

You don't list every conversation that influenced an RFC.

You don't footnote every person who helped you form an opinion.

Sometimes you don't even know where an idea came from anymore.

That doesn't mean attribution doesn't matter. Obviously it does. If I am knowingly borrowing someone's work, I should say so.

But authorship has never meant proving that every sentence emerged from my brain uncontaminated by another person's influence.

We are all composites of things we've read and heard and argued about.

AI makes that relationship much stranger because the influence can be immediate, prolific, and invisible.

That means I think I need to be *more* diligent.

Not necessarily that I need to pretend the tool wasn't involved.

## The dangerous part is when I stop noticing

One thing I do worry about is influence I don't recognize.

An LLM can take something I said and reorganize it in a way that nudges the argument somewhere I didn't intend.

It can make an idea sound more confident than I was.

It can introduce framing that feels natural enough that I don't immediately notice that it wasn't mine.

That is different from spell check.

Spell check isn't quietly changing the thesis.

So I don't think "it's just another writing tool" completely captures what is happening.

There is a responsibility here.

If I'm going to use these systems this heavily, part of the job is being suspicious of the output.

Does this actually represent what I said?

Do I believe this sentence?

Would I say this to another person?

Did the tool make this argument better, or did it change the argument?

That review feels like an important part of authorship to me.

The second I stop doing that and start parroting generated ideas because they sound plausible, the whole thing falls apart.

That's where it starts feeling like slop.

## Something else happened

The weirdest part of all this is that the biggest effect may not have been on my writing.

Months of constantly voice dictating to agents has made me much more practiced at expressing myself out loud.

That sounds obvious written down.

If you spend hours every week explaining what you think, you get better at explaining what you think.

But I didn't expect it to matter outside of work.

I've noticed it in conversations with my wife.

We've always talked. We've always gotten along well. But I have always carried that same weird fear that if I say an opinion out loud, I have somehow committed to it.

That can make exploration difficult.

Sometimes you need to say the wrong thing before you can figure out what the right thing is.

Sometimes you need to hear yourself explain something before you realize you don't actually believe the explanation.

Practicing that process in a place where I feel completely safe being wrong has made me more comfortable doing it with other people.

I feel more willing to say, "I think this, but I'm not sure."

I feel more comfortable changing my mind halfway through a conversation.

I feel less boxed in by something I said ten minutes ago.

And I think that has allowed me to have deeper conversations with people I care about.

I did not expect that.

## The opposite could happen

There is an obviously darker version of this.

Someone could take all of the things they used to work through with another person and move them into a conversation with an AI instead.

You can imagine slowly deciding that it is easier to talk about your fears, frustrations, relationships, and problems with a machine that never gets tired of listening.

That could be incredibly isolating.

A life where you become better at talking to an AI and worse at talking to people sounds miserable.

I don't want that.

I don't know that I completely understand where the line is yet.

My goal isn't to move all of my thinking into one place or the other.

I'm trying to figure out which parts benefit from happening privately, effectively inside my head but out loud, and which parts need another human being.

So far, for me, the private practice seems to be making the human conversations easier.

I want to keep paying attention to whether that stays true.

## AI makes this unusually uncomfortable

I understand why people are suspicious of AI-generated writing.

I am suspicious of plenty of it too.

There is an enormous amount of content being generated by people who don't seem particularly interested in what the content says.

There are companies flooding the internet with garbage.

There are real questions around labor, ownership, compensation, training data, economic displacement, and what happens when this technology becomes good enough to automate work that millions of people depend on.

Those questions bother me too.

Sometimes a lot.

But I don't think I need to settle every societal consequence of AI before I can ask whether this particular tool, used this particular way, is helping me express myself.

Those can both be true.

I can be deeply uncomfortable with where parts of this technology may take us and still find this specific use of it valuable.

I think collapsing those into one binary question — AI good or AI bad — prevents us from talking about either one particularly well.

## Do I need to tell you?

The practical question I keep arriving at is whether every post needs a disclaimer.

"AI assisted."

"Written with AI."

"Edited by an LLM."

Right now, I understand why people want that information.

The technology is new. There isn't a shared expectation around what "I used AI to write this" means.

That could mean I dictated every thought myself and used a model to organize them.

It could mean I wrote the entire post and asked for grammar fixes.

It could mean I entered a title and copied whatever came back.

Those are wildly different creative processes being described with the same sentence.

So I think transparency about my process is useful.

I'm just not convinced the correct form of transparency is a warning label attached to everything I ever write.

Maybe this post is the disclosure.

If someone asks whether I use AI when I write, the answer is yes.

This is how.

## Is this my voice?

So, back to the question.

Are these my words?

I think they are.

Not because I can prove that every sentence originated entirely inside my head.

I couldn't prove that before AI either.

They're mine because I recognize myself in them.

Because the ideas represent what I actually think.

Because I decided what stayed and what went.

Because I can explain why they're here.

Because if they're wrong, I'm the person responsible for being wrong.

And because when I read them back, I want to have that feeling:

That's me.

Those are my words.

That's what I believe.

At least for now.
